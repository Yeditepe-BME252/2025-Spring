* ($\S 2.1$) Preliminaries (Session 6)
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear appear appear ...)
- Change in a physical variable, $\phi$, is usually denoted by $d$ or $\partial$, is called a *differential* of $\phi$, such as $d\phi$ or $\partial\phi$.
- The choice of correct representation depends on how many other variables it depends on, e.g. if it depends on a single variable such as $\phi(t)$ then the *differential change in variable* $\phi$ with respect to a *differential change in time*, $dt$, is denoted as $$\frac{d\phi}{dt}$$
- When the physical variable depends on more than one independent variables such as $\phi(x_1, x_2)$, then the *differential change in* $\phi$ with respect to $x_1$ and $x_2$ are denoted as $$\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_1},\;\; \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_2}$$

** ($\S 2.1.1$) Points, Tensors
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear appear appear ...)
- In the remainder of the course a stationary (non-moving) coordinate frame $\{\boldsymbol{e}_i\}$ in Euclidean space $\mathbb{E}^3$ will be used.
- We identify points $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{E}^3$ with, $$ \boldsymbol{x} = x_i \boldsymbol{e}_i = \begin{Bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{Bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3$$
- We identify vectors $\boldsymbol{v} \in \mathcal{V}$ with, $$ \boldsymbol{v} = v_i \boldsymbol{e}_i = \begin{Bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{Bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3$$
- We identify second-order tensors $\boldsymbol{S} \in \mathcal{V}^2$ with, $$ \boldsymbol{S} = S_{ij} \boldsymbol{e}_i \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_j= \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\ S_{21} & S_{22} & S_{23} \\ S_{31} & S_{32} & S_{33} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$$
  
** ($\S 2.1.2$) Norms, Order
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear)
- By the standard (Euclidean) norms on the spaces $\mathbb{E}^3$, $\mathcal{V}$, and $\mathcal{V}^2$ we mean the scalar functions defined by $$\begin{align}|\boldsymbol{x}| &= \sqrt{\boldsymbol{x} \cdot \boldsymbol{x}}, \;\; \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{E}^3 \\|\boldsymbol{v} | &= \sqrt{\boldsymbol{v} \cdot \boldsymbol{v}}, \;\; \forall \boldsymbol{v} \in \mathcal{V} \\|\boldsymbol{S} | &= \sqrt{\boldsymbol{S} : \boldsymbol{S}}, \;\; \forall \boldsymbol{S} \in \mathcal{V}^2  \end{align}$$
- Consider a function $\boldsymbol{f}: U \rightarrow W$, where $U$ and $W$ denote any of the spaces $\mathbb{E}^3$, $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{V}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}$.\\
  If there are constants $C>0$ and $r>0$ such that $|\boldsymbol{f} (\boldsymbol{h})| \leq C|\boldsymbol{h}|^r$ as $\boldsymbol{h} \rightarrow 0$, we write $$\boldsymbol{f} (\boldsymbol{h}) = \mathcal{O}(|\boldsymbol{h}|^r)\;\; \mathrm{as} \;\; \boldsymbol{h} \rightarrow 0.$$

** ($\S 2.2.1$) Gradient of a Scalar Field 

#+ATTR_HTML: :width 1600px
[[../images/gradientofphi.jpg]]

*** Definition of Scalar Gradient
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear)
- Let $\{\boldsymbol{e}_i\}$ be an arbitrary frame. Then, the *scalar gradient*  corresponding to a field $\phi = \phi(\boldsymbol{x})$, can be written as $$\nabla \phi = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x_i} \boldsymbol{e}_i.$$ where $\phi : \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ , is a mapping from the coordinate space onto the set of real numbers.
- The derivative of a scalar field is a vector field, $\nabla \phi : \mathbb{E}^3 \rightarrow \mathcal{V}$.
- The derivative symbol may be replaced with shorthand alternatives $$\frac{\partial \Box}{\partial x_i} \quad \quad \partial_i \Box \quad \quad \Box_{,i}$$
- We can use the Taylor series expansion, for any small vector $d\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{V}$ to show $$\phi({x_i} + d{x_i}) = \phi({x_i}) + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i + \mathcal{O}(dx_i dx_i)$$
- In tensor notation the above indicial expression takes the form $$\phi(\boldsymbol{x} + d\boldsymbol{x}) = \phi(\boldsymbol{x}) + \nabla \phi \cdot d\boldsymbol{x} +  \mathcal{O} (|d\boldsymbol{x}|^2)$$

** ($\S 2.2.1$) Gradient of a Vector Field 

#+ATTR_HTML: :width 900px
[[../images/windy.mp4]]

#+REVEAL: split
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear)
- Let $\{\boldsymbol{e}_i\}$ be an arbitrary (Cartesian) frame and let $\boldsymbol{v} = v_i \boldsymbol{e}_i$ denote a vector field such that $\boldsymbol{v} : \mathbb{E}^3 \rightarrow \mathcal{V}$, then the *vector gradient* in indicial notation reads $$\nabla \boldsymbol{v} = \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i} \boldsymbol{e}_i \otimes \boldsymbol{e}_j$$
- The derivative of a vector field $\boldsymbol{v} : \mathbb{E}^3 \rightarrow \mathcal{V}$ is a second-order tensor field $\nabla \boldsymbol{v} : \mathbb{E}^3 \rightarrow \mathcal{V}^2$.
- The gradient in indicial form can be written in a variety of ways including $$\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial x_i} \quad \quad \partial_i v_j \quad \quad v_{j,i}$$
- We can use the Taylor series expansion, for any small vector $d\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{V}$ to show $${v}_i(\boldsymbol{x} + d\boldsymbol{x}) = {v}_i(\boldsymbol{x}) + \frac{\partial {v}_j}{\partial x_i}\,dx_j + \mathcal{O}(|d\boldsymbol{x}|^2)$$
- In tensor notation the above indicial expression takes the form $$\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x} + d\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x}) + \nabla \boldsymbol{v} \,d\boldsymbol{x} +  \mathcal{O} (|d\boldsymbol{x}|^2)$$

*** Examples from Mechanics
- The velocity gradient tensor $$\nabla\boldsymbol{v} = v_{j,i} \mathbf{e}_i \otimes \mathbf{e}_j$$

*** Example: Flow Through a Pipe
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear)
- *Case 1: Uniform Flow* \\
  \[\boldsymbol{v} = v_0 \mathbf{e}_x\]
  \[\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{v} = 0\]
  (incompressible).\\

- *Case 2: Expanding Flow* \\
  \[\boldsymbol{v} = kx \mathbf{e}_x\] 
  \[\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{v} = k > 0\]
  (expansion).\\

- *Case 3: Compressing Flow* \\
  \[\boldsymbol{v} = (k/y) \mathbf{e}_y\]
  \[\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{v} = -k/y^2 < 0\]
  (compression).

*** Examples from Mechanics
#+ATTR_REVEAL: :frag (appear)
- Conservation of Mass: $$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \boldsymbol{v} ) = 0$$
- Conservation of Linear Momentum: $$\rho \dot{\boldsymbol{v}} = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{S} + \rho \boldsymbol{b}$$
* ($\S2.2.3$) Curl
*Core Concept:*\\
The curl (\(\nabla \times \boldsymbol{v}\)) measures *local rotation* in a vector field, $\boldsymbol{v}$:
- Direction = Rotation axis (right-hand rule)
- Magnitude = Rotation strength

*** Key Example: Vorticity in Fluid Mechanics
**** Velocity Field
\(\boldsymbol{v} = (-y, x, 0)\) (counterclockwise rotating fluid)

**** Curl Calculation
\begin{equation*}
\nabla \times \boldsymbol{v} = 
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf{e}_x & \mathbf{e}_y & \mathbf{e}_z \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\
-y & x & 0
\end{vmatrix}
= 2\mathbf{e}_z
\end{equation*}

*** Physical Interpretation
1. Fluid rotates like a rigid body (\(\omega = 1\))
2. Vorticity \(\boldsymbol{\omega} = 2\mathbf{e}_z\) (twice the angular velocity)
3. Paddlewheel test: Spins due to nonzero curl

*** Why It Matters
- <<<Fluid Dynamics>>>: Predicts vortices/turbulence
- <<<Solid Mechanics>>>: Reveals local twisting in deformations
- <<<Field Theory>>>: Distinguishes conservative vs. rotational fields
